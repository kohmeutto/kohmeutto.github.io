Abstract

The discretization of nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs) involving material discontinuities and advection components induces Jacobian operator non-normality. This non-normality causes spectral drift and convergence degradation in nonlinear solvers. Introducing empirical relaxation factors or numerical diffusion errors for system stabilization alters the mathematical consistency of the discretization. Securing geometric regularity based on the Graph Laplacian is insufficient to resolve the non-orthogonality of the eigenvector basis. Excluding empirical parameter tuning, this study proposes a numerical procedure to control advective asymmetry via algebraic projection within the linear system.

Defining the residue transformation of the linear operator algebraically isolates the first-order advection residual embedded within the Fréchet derivative. Mapping the isolated continuous residual to the discrete space derives a local asymmetry indicator based on the discrete commutator error. This indicator is formulated into an overdetermined linear system based on the edge-node incidence matrix functioning as a discrete gradient operator. Applying the discrete Helmholtz-Hodge decomposition (DHHD) separates and subtracts the anti-symmetric component of the effective Jacobian. This subtraction constructs the algebraic preconditioner.

The proposed algebraic preconditioning is verified in one-dimensional and two-dimensional singular perturbation systems using the method of manufactured solutions (MMS). Numerical experiments indicate that under limit conditions imposing material discontinuity and nonlinearity, the algebraic preconditioning demonstrates condition number stabilization compared to the unconditioned Jacobian. Correcting the cross-coupling asymmetry within the nonlinear system via linear algebraic operations satisfies the asymptotic quadratic convergence of the finite volume method (FVM). This procedure avoids introducing additional numerical dissipation terms.

1. INTRODUCTION

Nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs) develop state variable gradients in regions containing boundary layers, shock waves, and material discontinuities. When such systems are linearized via projection into discrete spaces, such as the finite volume method (FVM), the Jacobian operator exhibits structural non-normality due to convective components [1, 2]. The spectrum of a non-normal operator yields a non-orthogonal eigenvector basis, causing transient growth of perturbations even when eigenvalues reside in the stable region of the complex plane [3, 4]. This ill-conditioning reduces the convergence speed of Krylov subspace methods and contributes to the divergence of nonlinear iterative solvers [5, 6, 7]. To suppress discrete commutator errors arising from the effective drift velocity divergence during the discretization of continuous conservation laws into non-uniform meshes, r-adaptive topological mapping techniques are employed [8, 9, 10, 11]. Specifically, adaptive grid generation based on the Graph Laplacian and harmonic mapping serves to disperse mesh distortion and prevent tangling [12]. However, geometric regularity alone is insufficient to eliminate the intrinsic asymmetry of the Jacobian induced by convective terms, necessitating explicit preconditioning to control spectral distortion [13, 14].

Therefore, this study proposes a numerical technique to control convective asymmetry through linear algebraic operations without requiring empirical parameter tuning or additional numerical dissipation. A residual projection of linear operators is introduced to isolate first-order convective residuals within the Jacobian. Using the isolated residuals, local asymmetry indicators are derived to guide geometric topological mapping via the Graph Laplacian. This study analytically proves that the algebraic projection vector extracted from the cross-diagonal component ratio is equivalent to the geometric volume ratio of the discrete FVM domain and the one-dimensional line integral of the drift velocity. Integrating this formulation with the discrete Helmholtz-Hodge decomposition (DHHD), a preconditioner is designed to isolate the anti-symmetric components of the effective Jacobian into the residual space and symmetrize the search domain of the linear solver [15].

To verify the mathematical consistency and discretization order of the proposed algebraic correction technique, error evaluation is performed using the method of manufactured solutions (MMS) [16, 17, 18]. It is confirmed that the algorithm suppresses divergence due to floating-point errors and satisfies the theoretical asymptotic second-order convergence even under conditions of material discontinuity where analytical solutions are absent. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the residue mapping of non-normal operators and Fréchet derivative expansion. Section 3 establishes the algebraic preconditioning procedure based on discrete commutator errors. Section 4 describes numerical results for one-dimensional and two-dimensional singular perturbation systems.

2. RESIDUAL PROJECTION OF NON-NORMAL OPERATORS AND FRÉCHET DERIVATIVE EXPANSION

This section algebraically analyzes the factors increasing the condition number of the Jacobian matrix in discrete spaces. The residual projection of a linear operator $L$ is defined. Using this projection, the weak form of the first-order advection operator, which contributes to asymmetry, is derived via the Fréchet derivative.

2.1 Algebraic residual projection

To analyze the algebraic characteristics of differential operators, an inner product $\langle\phi,\psi\rangle=\int_{\Omega}\phi^{*}\psi d\Omega$ is defined for state functions $\phi$ and $\psi$ within a complex Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$.

The superscript $*$ denotes the complex conjugate. Preserving the relationship between a differential operator and its formal adjoint during the discretization of PDEs is a prerequisite for ensuring physical conservation laws and numerical stability [19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. When global self-adjointness is not satisfied due to convective terms, the residual projection $\mathcal{R}$ is defined to quantify the difference between an operator $L$ and its formal adjoint $L^\dagger$:

$$ \mathcal{R}(L) \equiv L - L^\dagger \quad\text{(1)} $$

The expansion of the weak form in the inner product space is as follows:

$$ \langle \phi, \mathcal{R}(L) \psi \rangle \equiv \langle \phi, (L - L^\dagger) \psi \rangle = \langle \phi, L \psi \rangle - \langle \phi, L^\dagger \psi \rangle \quad\text{(2)} $$

The residue operator preserves the additivity and homogeneity of linear operators. For scalar coefficients $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$ and operators $A$ and $B$, the following holds:

$$ \mathcal{R}(\alpha A + \beta B) = \alpha \mathcal{R}(A) + \beta \mathcal{R}(B) \quad\text{(3)} $$

When a discretized system for PDEs includes convective components, the Jacobian operator exhibits structural non-normality. The spectrum of such a non-normal operator yields a non-orthogonal basis [3, 4], inducing transient growth of perturbations during Newton iterations. Consequently, the residual projection serves as an algebraic diagnostic tool for factors increasing non-normality, which eigenvalue analysis alone cannot capture.

2.2 Fréchet derivative expansion and algebraic separation of first-order convective residuals

The Jacobian $J = \partial F / \partial u$ of the governing conservation equation $F(u) = D(p(u)Du) - q(u) = 0$ is expanded [24, 25, 26]. Here, $D$ denotes an abstract spatial differential operator, and $q(u)$ represents a nonlinear source term depending on the state variable $u$. Given a small perturbation $\delta u$ in the state variable $u$, the Jacobian operator $J$ is identified by linearizing the governing equation $F(u) = 0$ around the state $u$. By retaining the first-order terms in the expansion of the increment $F(u + \delta u) - F(u)$, the action of $J$ on a perturbation $\delta u$ is derived as follows:

$$ J \delta u = \underbrace{D(p(u) D\delta u)}_{L_2} + \underbrace{D \left( \frac{\partial p}{\partial u} Du \delta u \right)}_{L_1} \underbrace{- \frac{\partial q}{\partial u} \delta u}_{L_0} \quad\text{(3)} $$

The effective drift velocity $v \equiv (\partial p/\partial u)(Du)$ is defined as the product of the state variable gradient $Du$ and the rate of change in material properties $\partial p/\partial u$. The first-order term transforms into an advection operator $L_1 = D(v \cdot)$ [27, 28]. The residual projection is applied to $J = L_2 + L_1 + L_0$:

$$ \mathcal{R}(J) = \mathcal{R}(L_2) + \mathcal{R}(L_1) + \mathcal{R}(L_0) \quad\text{(4)} $$

Each term is evaluated via integration by parts within the space of compactly supported test functions $C_c^\infty(\Omega)$. The zeroth-order term $L_0 = -\partial q/\partial u$ and the second-order term $L_2 = D(pD \cdot)$ possess formal self-adjointness. Their residual projections vanish, yielding $\mathcal{R}(L_0) = 0$ and $\mathcal{R}(L_2) = 0$. Consequently, the non-normality of the Jacobian depends on the first-order advection operator $L_1$. Since integration by parts yields $L_1^\dagger = -vD$, the residual projection expands as follows:

$$ \mathcal{R}(L_1)\psi = (L_1 - L_1^\dagger)\psi = D(v\psi) - (-vD\psi) = D(v\psi) + v(D\psi) \quad\text{(5)} $$

Applying the Leibniz rule $D(v\psi) = (Dv)\psi + v(D\psi)$ yields:

$$ \mathcal{R}(L_1)\psi = \left[ (Dv)\psi + v(D\psi) \right] + v(D\psi) = (Dv)\psi + 2v(D\psi) \quad\text{(6)} $$

Expressing this in the weak form, the convective residual inside the nonlinear Jacobian matrix is identified:

$$ \langle \phi, \mathcal{R}(J)\psi \rangle_\Omega = \langle \phi, \left[ (Dv)\psi + 2v(D\psi) \right] \rangle_\Omega \quad\text{(7)} $$

This result indicates that the combination of the effective drift velocity divergence $Dv$ and the homogeneous advection $2vD$ induces the system asymmetry. This derivation provides the basis for analyzing discrete commutator errors in Section 3.

3. DISCRETE COMMUTATOR ERROR AND ALGEBRAIC PRECONDITIONING

This section projects the weak form of the convective residuals derived in Section 2 into a discrete linear algebraic system. Using this projection, an r-adaptive topological mapping is performed, and a symmetric preconditioner is designed to control system asymmetry.

3.1 Derivation of local asymmetry indicator based on discrete commutator error

The target of discretization is the component $(Dv)\psi$, representing the effective drift velocity divergence acting on the state function. To control numerical oscillations during point-wise evaluation, the relation $D(v\psi) = (Dv)\psi + v(D\psi)$ is applied inversely:

$$ (Dv)\psi = D(v\psi) - v(D\psi) \quad\text{(8)} $$

The total discrete differential summation at node $k$ for the state function $\psi$ and $v$ is expressed as $[D(v\psi)]_k = \sum_{j} \hat{D}_{kj} (v_j \psi_j)$. Due to the properties of the discrete differential operator, the sum of the weights in each row equals zero, yielding $\sum_j \hat{D}_{kj} = 0$. Thus, $v_k \psi_k \sum_j \hat{D}_{kj} = 0$ holds. Subtracting this term from the initial summation yields:

$$ [D(v\psi)]_k = \sum_{j} \hat{D}_{kj} (v_j \psi_j - v_k \psi_k) \quad\text{(9)} $$

The equation is separated by applying the algebraic identity $v_j \psi_j - v_k \psi_k = (v_j - v_k)\psi_j + v_k(\psi_j - \psi_k)$:

$$ [D(v\psi)]_k = \sum_{j} (v_j - v_k) \hat{D}_{kj} \psi_j + \sum_{j} v_k \hat{D}_{kj} (\psi_j - \psi_k) \quad\text{(10)} $$

The second summation term expands to $\sum_j v_k \hat{D}_{kj} \psi_j - v_k \psi_k \sum_j \hat{D}_{kj}$ and reduces to $\sum_j v_k \hat{D}_{kj} \psi_j$ via the zero-sum condition. This represents the discrete form of the advection term $v(D\psi)$. Consequently, the discrete projection of $(Dv)\psi$ yields the first summation term:

$$ (Dv)\psi = \sum_{j} (v_j - v_k) \hat{D}_{kj} \psi_j \quad\text{(11)} $$

Substituting and combining the discrete forms of the continuous residuals $(Dv)\psi + 2v(D\psi)$ yields the local directional tension $\mathcal{T}_{kj}$ acting on node $k$:

$$ \mathcal{T}_{kj} = (v_j + v_k) \hat{D}_{kj} \psi_j \quad\text{(12)} $$

The cross-tension indicator $S_{kj}$ between nodes is defined as the arithmetic mean of their absolute values to prevent directional cancellation:

$$ S_{kj} = \frac{1}{2} \left( |\mathcal{T}_{kj}| + |\mathcal{T}_{jk}| \right) \quad\text{(13)} $$

A dimensionless indicator $\bar{S}_{kj} = S_{kj} / \max_{m,n}(S_{mn})$, normalized by the maximum tension indicator $\max_{m,n}(S_{mn})$ across the discrete domain, is introduced to construct the weight matrix $W_{kj} = 1.0 + \bar{S}_{kj}$. This weight $W_{kj}$ acts as the local stiffness of a spring connecting two adjacent nodes, treating the discrete mesh as a virtual spring network. The equilibrium state, minimizing the total strain energy of the system, is determined by the condition that the sum of tensions acting on each internal node $i$ equals a zero vector:

$$ \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} W_{ij} (\mathbf{X}_j - \mathbf{X}_i) = \mathbf{0} \quad\text{(14)} $$

Expanding these local equilibrium conditions to a global system yields an algebraic system via the Graph Laplacian:

$$ \hat{L}_{sys} = \hat{K} - \hat{W} \quad\text{(15(a))} $$$$ \tilde{L}_{sys} \mathbf{X}_{new} = \mathbf{B}_{ref} $$

Here, $\hat{W}$ is the global adjacency weight matrix composed of the local stiffness elements $W_{ij}$. The diagonal matrix $\hat{K}$ denotes a degree matrix, representing the sum of adjacent weights $K_{ii} = \sum_j W_{ij}$ for each node. $\tilde{L}_{sys}$ represents a modified operator, imposing Dirichlet boundary conditions onto the pure Laplacian $\hat{L}_{sys}$. The vector $\mathbf{B}_{ref}$ contains the fixed boundary coordinate values. Each node volume $\tilde{V}_k$ within the derived coordinate system $\mathbf{X}_{new}$ is recalculated, yielding the dual volume metric $\hat{G}$:

$$ \hat{G} = \text{diag}(\tilde{V}_0, \tilde{V}_1, \dots, \tilde{V}_{N-1}) $$

This formulation performs harmonic mapping by integrating the local indicator, derived from commutator errors, into a weighted Graph Laplacian system. Consequently, nodes concentrate in regions with error gradients, while algebraically preserving the positivity of the Jacobian determinant during discrete time integration [29]. This distribution prevents mesh tangling and maintains geometric regularity, even under singular perturbation conditions.

3.2 Symmetric preconditioner design based on algebraic projection

The derived geometric metric $\hat{G}$ and the diagonal matrix $\hat{C}$ are applied to the residual vector $F(u)$, yielding a new system $\tilde{F}(u) = \hat{C}\hat{G}F(u) = 0$. The first-order approximation for a state variable perturbation $u + \delta u$ expands as follows:

$$ \tilde{F}(u+\delta u) \approx \tilde{F}(u) + \left( \frac{\partial\hat{C}}{\partial u}\hat{G}F(u) + \hat{C}\frac{\partial\hat{G}}{\partial u}F(u) \right) \delta u + \hat{C}\hat{G}\hat{J}\delta u $$

As Newton iterations progress and $F(u) \to 0$, the effective Jacobian approximates to $\tilde{J} = \hat{C}\hat{G}\hat{J}$, where $\hat{J}$ denotes the original Jacobian. To symmetrize $\tilde{J}$, a magnitude preservation constraint $|\tilde{J}_{ij}| = |\tilde{J}_{ji}|$ is imposed on the cross-diagonal components. Component-wise expansion requires the following equality:

$$ c_i |(\hat{G}\hat{J})_{ij}| = c_j |(\hat{G}\hat{J})_{ji}| $$

The diagonal components of the matrix $\hat{C}$ are defined as $c_i = e^{x_i}$ to satisfy the positivity condition. Taking the natural logarithm of both sides yields the algebraic difference $b_{ij}$, defining a vector indicator on the edge connecting adjacent nodes $i$ and $j$:

$$ \ln(c_i) - \ln(c_j) = \ln\left( \frac{|(\hat{G}\hat{J})_{ji}|}{|(\hat{G}\hat{J})_{ij}|} \right) \equiv b_{ij} $$

For the substitution variable $\phi_i = \ln(c_i)$, the above equation is an overdetermined linear system of the form $\hat{S}\boldsymbol{\phi} = \mathbf{b}$. The matrix $\hat{S}$ is the edge-node incidence matrix representing the topological connectivity of the discrete mesh. It functions as a discrete gradient operator with elements of $\{-1, 1, 0\}$ depending on the edge direction. The $\phi_i$ derived through this represents a global potential field obtained by spatially integrating the irrotational component of the local asymmetry indicators $b_{ij}$ scattered across each edge. After imposing the reference point condition on the Dirichlet boundary nodes, the solution $\boldsymbol{\phi}_{LS}$ is calculated through the least squares method to determine $\hat{C}$. The DHHD applied to analyze the linear system and the analysis of the cancellation efficiency of the asymmetric components are described in Appendix A.

3.3 Proof of geometric and physical equivalence of the algebraic projection vector

The indicator $b_{ij}$ represents the local flux imbalance induced by the multidimensional effective drift velocity vector $\mathbf{v}$ and geometric volume ratio within $\hat{J}$. The residual equation $F_i = 0$ for the expression $-p\nabla \psi + \mathbf{v}\psi$ within the continuous Jacobian operator expands into a surface integral over the surface area $\partial \Omega_i$ of node $i$ via the FVM [30]:

$$ \iint_{\partial \Omega_i} (-p \nabla \psi + \mathbf{v} \psi) \cdot \mathbf{n} dA = \sum_{j} \Gamma_{i \to j} $$

When the unit direction vector of the edge is $\mathbf{e}_{ij}$ and the one-dimensional coordinate is $\xi$, the one-dimensional directional derivative for the cross-sectional area $A_{ij}$ is $\Gamma_{i \to j} = A_{ij} ( -p(\xi)d\psi/d\xi + v(\xi)\psi )$. Here, $v(\xi) = \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{e}_{ij}$. Since the source term is absent inside the edge, the corresponding ordinary differential equation equals a constant $C_0$ independent of space. It is replaced with a complete differential form by applying an integrating factor:

$$ \frac{C_0}{p(\xi)} \exp\left(-\int_{\xi_i}^\xi \frac{v(s)}{p(s)} ds\right) = -\frac{d}{d\xi} \left[ \psi(\xi) \exp\left(-\int_{\xi_i}^\xi \frac{v(s)}{p(s)} ds\right) \right] $$

Integrating over the interval $[\xi_i, \xi_j]$ yields a relational expression for the node state variables:

$$ C_0 = \frac{1}{R_{ij}} \psi_i - \frac{1}{R_{ij}}\exp\left(-\int_{\xi_i}^{\xi_j} \frac{v(s)}{p(s)} ds\right) \psi_j $$

The effective geometric resistance $R_{ij}$ is defined as follows:

$$ R_{ij} \equiv \int_{\xi_i}^{\xi_j} \frac{1}{p(\xi)} \exp\left(-\int_{\xi_i}^\xi \frac{v(s)}{p(s)} ds\right) d\xi $$

The cross-coupling component of the discrete Jacobian matrix $\hat{J}$ is $\hat{J}_{ij} = \partial F_i / \partial \psi_j$. This is substituted into the algebraic projection vector $b_{ij}$ multiplied by the diagonal metric $\hat{G}$:

$$ b_{ij} \equiv \ln \left( \frac{\tilde{V}_j |\hat{J}_{ji}|}{\tilde{V}_i |\hat{J}_{ij}|} \right) $$

The cross-sectional area and geometric resistance $R_{ij}$ cancel out due to the area symmetry $A_{ij} = A_{ji}$. Consequently, the vector $b_{ij}$ reduces to the sum of the geometric volume ratio and the one-dimensional line integral of the Péclet field:

$$ b_{ij} = \ln\left(\frac{\tilde{V}_j}{\tilde{V}_i}\right) + \int_{\xi_i}^{\xi_j} \frac{v(s)}{p(s)} ds \equiv \ln\left(\frac{\tilde{V}_j}{\tilde{V}_i}\right) + \int_i^j \left(\frac{\mathbf{v}}{p}\right) \cdot d\mathbf{l} $$

3.4 Isolation of anti-symmetric components of the effective Jacobian and search matrix construction

$\tilde{J}$ decomposes into a symmetric component $\tilde{H} = (\tilde{J} + \tilde{J}^T)/2$ and an anti-symmetric component $\tilde{A}_{raw} = (\tilde{J} - \tilde{J}^T)/2$. To isolate the anti-symmetric component $\tilde{A}_{int}$ corresponding to the internal domain, the matrix is pre- and post-multiplied by a diagonal matrix $\hat{M}_s$, assigning 0 to boundary nodes and 1 to internal nodes:

$$ \tilde{A}_{int} = \hat{M}_s \tilde{A}_{raw} \hat{M}_s $$

While the intrinsic asymmetric component $\tilde{A}_{int}$ is preserved within the residual $F(u)$ on the right-hand side of the governing equation, it is algebraically subtracted to formulate the preconditioner $\tilde{J}_{pure}$, serving as the search matrix for the linear solver:

$$ \tilde{J}_{pure} = \tilde{J} - \tilde{A}_{int} $$

This formulation employs an inexact Newton method using the symmetric preconditioner $\tilde{J}_{pure}$ as the search matrix [31, 32]. As the linearization system $\tilde{J}_{pure} \delta u = -\tilde{F}(u)$ reaches the convergence state $\delta u \to 0$, the physical residual $F(u^*) = 0$ holds identically. The analytical proof ensuring convergence, by demonstrating that the spectrum of the formulated preconditioner resides within a bounded complex disk, is detailed in Appendix B.

4. NUMERICAL EXPERIMENTS AND VERIFICATION

To evaluate the performance of the algebraic control and the order of numerical discretization, this section derives the forcing source term $q(x)$ through inverse calculation using MMS. MMS serves as a verification tool to identify the accumulation of discretization errors and logical defects in implementation for PDEs lacking analytical solutions or containing material discontinuities [33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39]. This study confirms that FVM consistently maintains its intrinsic asymptotic convergence of $\mathcal{O}(\Delta x^2)$, even under conditions applying the algebraic preconditioning.

The proposed algebraic preconditioning involves explicit algebraic control, from the assembly of nonlinear operators to the extraction of anti-symmetric components. Consequently, the algorithm separates the control and linear algebra layers, implementing them directly in a 64-bit double-precision environment and excluding general-purpose automatic differentiation libraries or commercial PDE packages. The specific geometric mapping and algebraic projection procedures are as follows.

The grid movement control stage performs r-adaptive grid relocation based on $S_{kj}$ derived in Section 3.1. A weighted Graph Laplacian system is constructed by incorporating the geometric characteristics of the regions where errors occur into the weight matrix $\hat{W}$ to derive the harmonic mapping equations [40, 41]. Auto-snapping is applied to nodes adjacent to interfaces to prevent the harmonic mean paradox and algebraic distortion that occur when a material interface intersects a single element. If a discrete node approaches a physical interface within a distance of less than half the element length, the coordinates of that node are adjusted to the interface position. This suppresses the occurrence of kinks inside the element and controls the accidental error cancellation that occurs when the initial grid aligns with the interface.

To control the increase in residuals caused by geometric deformation during the mesh relocation process, an optimal state rollback technique is applied [42]. The norm of the nonlinear residual is calculated at each iteration step, and the geometric coordinates and state variables representing the minimum error are stored. If the error increases after grid movement, the system is restored to the previous state and the topological deformation of the mesh is frozen to induce asymptotic convergence. The assembly of the nonlinear residual $F(u)$ and the Fréchet derivative $\hat{J}$ is based on analytical differentiation rules. Spatial integration utilizes multi-point Gauss-Legendre quadrature in 1D domains and edge-midpoint evaluation in 2D unstructured meshes [23].

The algebraic normalization stage derives a diagonal normalization matrix $\hat{C}$ via the least-squares method to minimize internal residuals, satisfying the adjoint consistency condition of Section 3.2 [43]. A mask matrix $\hat{M}_s$, functioning as a projection operator, controls the influence of Dirichlet boundary conditions on the internal operator structure. Using this projection, the internal numerical anti-symmetric component $\tilde{A}_{int}$ is separated from the system asymmetry. Subtracting this component from the raw Jacobian yields the preconditioned Jacobian $\tilde{J}_{pure}$. A UMFPACK-based direct sparse solver solves the linear system $\tilde{J}_{pure} \delta u = -\tilde{F}(u)$. The condition number $\text{Cond}(CJ)$ presented in the subsequent tables denotes the condition number of the search matrix $\tilde{J}_{pure}$, formulated via the application of the geometric metric and the separation of the numerical anti-symmetric component.

4.1 Algebraic verification of 1D singular perturbation system

A strongly elliptic conservation equation with a state-dependent nonlinear diffusion coefficient $p(u, x)$ is defined on a dimensionless one-dimensional Euclidean domain $\Omega = [0, 1]$:

$$ \frac{d}{dx} \left( p(u, x) \frac{du}{dx} \right) = q(x) \quad \text{in } \Omega $$

The domain is divided into a left medium and a right medium at the interface $x_{int} = 0.2$, imposing a discontinuity on the diffusion coefficient $p(u, x)$. The interface is located at an asymmetric position to eliminate geometric dependence, preventing the accidental cancellation of interpolation errors that occurs when the initial uniform grid aligns with the interface. A diffusion contrast coefficient $\sigma$ is applied to the right medium:

$$ p(u, x) = \begin{cases} 1 + u^2 & \text{if } x \le x_{int} \\ \sigma (1 + u^2) & \text{if } x > x_{int} \end{cases} $$

The exact analytical solution $u_{exact}(x)$ for the MMS is constructed as follows, utilizing a sinusoidal wave with a wavenumber $k = 2\pi f$, where $f$ denotes the spatial frequency, as the basis function:

$$ u_{exact}(x) = \begin{cases} \sin(k x) + C_L x & \text{if } x \le x_{int} \\ \sin(k x) + C_R (x - 1) + u_{end} & \text{if } x > x_{int} \end{cases} $$

The first-order coefficients $C_L$ and $C_R$ are constants derived to satisfy the potential continuity $u_L = u_R$ and the nonlinear flux continuity $p_L u_L' = p_R u_R'$ at the interface $x_{int}$, where the subscripts $L$ and $R$ denote the limits as $x \to x_{int}$ from the left and right domains, respectively. The target potential at the right boundary $x=1$ is fixed at $u_{end} = 1.0$. An asymmetric Robin mixed boundary condition, expressed as $\alpha u + \beta p(u,x)du/dx = \gamma$ with $\alpha=2.0$ and $\beta=1.0$, is imposed on this right boundary. This condition degrades the diagonal dominance and diffuses non-normality throughout the system during the assembly of the Jacobian matrix.

Extending the one-dimensional verification, the proposed algebraic preconditioning is verified on a two-dimensional unstructured triangular mesh featuring high geometric degrees of freedom, accompanied by a cross-derivative term along the area vector direction. The nonlinear elliptic PDE defined on the two-dimensional domain $\Omega = [0, 1] \times [0, 1]$ is expressed as follows:

$$ \nabla \cdot \left( p(u) \nabla u \right) = q(x, y) \quad \text{in } \Omega $$

An exponential nonlinear diffusion coefficient $p(u) = \exp(k_{nl} u)$ is introduced to algebraically induce non-normality. The domain is divided into a left medium and a right medium at the vertical interface $x_{int} = 0.7$, applying $\sigma$ to the right medium:

$$ p(u, x, y) = \begin{cases} \exp(k_{nl} u) & \text{if } x \le x_{int} \\ \sigma \exp(k_{nl} u) & \text{if } x > x_{int} \end{cases} $$

The following 2D exponential decay model was applied as the analytical exact solution for MMS:

$$ u_{exact}(x, y) = \begin{cases} u_0 \exp(-\alpha_L x) \cos(\pi y) & \text{if } x \le x_{int} \\ u_0 \exp(-\alpha_L x_{int}) \exp(-\alpha_R (x - x_{int})) \cos(\pi y) & \text{if } x > x_{int} \end{cases} $$

As the exponential nonlinear term cancels out via the continuity condition $u_L = u_R$, the right decay rate derives as $\alpha_R = \alpha_L / \sigma$, inversely proportional to the diffusion contrast. An asymmetric Robin mixed boundary condition, specifying $\alpha=2.0$ and $\beta=1.0$, is imposed on the right and top boundaries to diffuse the cross-coupling asymmetry throughout the system.

During this process, the area determinant of the triangular elements is inspected. Node movements causing element inversion or negative areas are rejected, preserving the geometric regularity of the Delaunay triangular network.

5. Conclusion and Future Works

This study identifies the algebraic origin of the Jacobian operator non-normality and the discrete commutator error arising during the discretization of nonlinear PDEs. A numerical procedure is established to control these errors. Introducing the residue transformation $\mathcal{R}(L) \equiv L - L^\dagger$ of a linear operator, the first-order advection operator within the Fréchet derivative is defined as the effective drift velocity. Weak-form expansion demonstrates that the system asymmetry originates from the combination of the effective drift velocity divergence $Dv$ and the homogeneous advection $2vD$. These components are algebraically isolated. Mapping the isolated continuous advection residual to the discrete space derives a local inter-node tension indicator based on the discrete commutator error. Projecting this indicator onto a weighted Graph Laplacian system establishes an r-adaptive topological mapping responding to the error gradient to control mesh tangling. An algebraic projection vector is extracted from the cross-diagonal preservation constraint of the effective Jacobian. This algebraic projection vector is analytically equivalent to the sum of the geometric volume ratio in the FVM space and the one-dimensional line integral of the Péclet field. Applying the DHHD to the algebraic projection vector isolates the intrinsic anti-symmetric component of the effective Jacobian. Subtracting this component completes the algebraic preconditioner, symmetrizing the search matrix for the linear solver. The algebraic preconditioning is verified using the MMS in one-dimensional and two-dimensional singular perturbation systems. Numerical experiments show that within the discretization limit space imposing $\sigma=10^{-3}$ and $k_{nl}=4.0$, the algebraic preconditioning restricts the condition number growth of the unconditioned Jacobian, yielding a stabilization ratio of $968.9$. Controlling the residual asymmetry of the discrete space within the machine precision limit ensures the spectral convergence of the Krylov solver. This confirms the theoretical asymptotic quadratic convergence $\mathcal{O}(\Delta x^2)$ without artificial numerical dissipation.

The residue transformation and algebraic preconditioning established here derive from the vanishing condition $\mathcal{R}(J) = 0$ inside the strongly elliptic conservation system. Based on this structural analysis of operators, future work extends this numerical procedure to more complex PDE systems. For advection-dominated advection-diffusion systems, an orthogonal decomposition is required to separate the physical anti-symmetric components in continuous space from the numerical truncation errors caused by discrete grid non-uniformity. This separation provides an algebraic basis for a structure-preserving spatial discretization that selectively controls numerical errors while preserving physical fluxes. Additionally, extensions to energy-conserving systems, such as pure hyperbolic wave equations lacking diffusion terms, are considered. In this context, deriving quantitative diagnostic indicators for numerical dissipation and dispersion during time integration, based on the discrete commutator error, serves as a criterion to evaluate the physical conservation of high-order wave equation discretization schemes. Finally, beyond steady-state systems, further research on algebraic preconditioning for spatio-temporal Jacobian systems utilizing fully implicit time integration is required. Designing a spatio-temporal preconditioner that suppresses the eigenvalue drift of nonlinear systems due to time-step variations and ensures algebraic stability contributes to the analysis of complex multiphysics systems.

Appendix A. Graph Laplacian and Discrete Helmholtz-Hodge Decomposition

The normal equation is formulated to determine the least-squares solution $\boldsymbol{\phi}_{LS}$ minimizing the objective function of the overdetermined linear system $\hat{S}\boldsymbol{\phi} = \mathbf{b}$:

$$ (\hat{S}^T \hat{S}) \boldsymbol{\phi}_{LS} = \hat{S}^T \mathbf{b} $$

The matrix $\hat{S}^T \hat{S}$ denotes the unweighted topological Graph Laplacian operator. Based on the DHHD, an arbitrary edge vector $\mathbf{b}$ decomposes orthogonally as follows [42, 44, 45]:

$$ \mathbf{b} = \hat{S} \boldsymbol{\phi}_{LS} + \mathbf{r} $$

The projected component $\hat{S} \boldsymbol{\phi}_{LS}$ represents the conservative irrotational component, mapping to an algebraic scalar potential field. The residual $\mathbf{r}$ signifies the circulation component belonging to the matrix left null space, satisfying the orthogonality condition $\hat{S}^T \mathbf{r} = 0$.

Given the orthogonality of the two vectors, the Pythagorean theorem yields $\| \mathbf{b} \|_2^2 = \| \hat{S}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{LS} \|_2^2 + \| \mathbf{r} \|_2^2$. This relation derives the cancellation ratio $\eta$, quantifying the resolution of the conservative asymmetric components within the system:

$$ \eta = \frac{\|\hat{S}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{LS}\|_2^2}{\|\mathbf{b}\|_2^2} = 1 - \frac{\|\mathbf{r}\|_2^2}{\|\mathbf{b}\|_2^2} $$

Appendix B. Spectral Bound and Convergence Proof of the Preconditioned Operator

The eigenvalue of the target operator $\tilde{J}_{pure}^{-1} \tilde{J}$ is defined as $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$, with the corresponding eigenvector $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{C}^n$. The operator comprises the sum of a symmetric component $\tilde{H}$ and an anti-symmetric component, decomposing into $\tilde{J} = \tilde{H} + \tilde{A}_{raw}$ and $\tilde{J}_{pure} = \tilde{H} + \tilde{A}_{bnd}$, where $\tilde{A}_{bnd} = \tilde{A}_{raw} - \tilde{A}_{int}$. Substituting these into the generalized eigenvalue problem $\tilde{J} \mathbf{w} = \lambda \tilde{J}_{pure} \mathbf{w}$ yields:

$$ (\tilde{J}_{pure} + \tilde{A}_{int}) \mathbf{w} = \lambda \tilde{J}_{pure} \mathbf{w} $$$$ \tilde{A}_{int} \mathbf{w} = (\lambda - 1) \tilde{J}_{pure} \mathbf{w} $$

Pre-multiplying both sides by the conjugate transpose of the eigenvector $\mathbf{w}^*$ under the normalization condition $\|\mathbf{w}\| = 1$ constructs a quadratic form. The positive definite property ensures $\mathbf{w}^* \tilde{H} \mathbf{w} = h > 0$, while the anti-symmetry defines $\mathbf{w}^*\tilde{A}_{int} \mathbf{w} = i\mu$ and $\mathbf{w}^*\tilde{A}_{bnd} \mathbf{w} = i\nu$ for real numbers $\mu, \nu \in \mathbb{R}$. Substituting these relations yields:

$$ i\mu = (\lambda - 1)(h + i\nu) $$

Expanding this equation derives $\lambda - 1 = i\mu / (h + i\nu)$, followed by the derivation of the eigenvalue center shift magnitude $|\lambda - 1|^2$:

$$ |\lambda - 1|^2 = \frac{\mu^2}{h^2 + \nu^2} $$

Since $\nu^2 \ge 0$ in the denominator, the following inequality holds:

$$ |\lambda - 1| \le \frac{|\mu|}{h} = \frac{|\mathbf{w}^* \tilde{A}_{int} \mathbf{w}|}{\mathbf{w}^* \tilde{H} \mathbf{w}} $$

Defining the relative continuity constant of the anti-symmetric operator $\tilde{A}_{int}$ with respect to the symmetric operator $\tilde{H}$ as $\gamma_{int} \equiv \sup_{\mathbf{w} \ne 0} \{|\mathbf{w}^* \tilde{A}_{int} \mathbf{w}|/(\mathbf{w}^* \tilde{H} \mathbf{w})\}$, the spectrum $\Lambda(\tilde{J}_{pure}^{-1} \tilde{J})$ of the preconditioned operator resides within the following inequality set:

$$ \Lambda(\tilde{J}_{pure}^{-1} \tilde{J}) \subset \{ \lambda \in \mathbb{C} : |\lambda - 1| \le \gamma_{int} \} $$

This indicates that the proposed preconditioner clusters the eigenvalue distribution of the linearized operator within a bounded disk on the complex plane, algebraically guaranteeing the linear convergence of the Krylov solver [46].
